Plain-language answers about what carrydown does with your documents, your staffing notes and your engineers' coding agents, and what it cannot do. Each answer carries one of three labels: **supported** (implemented, documented and tested), **supported with prerequisites** (implemented and tested, but needs configuration your organization provides), or **planned** (the design exists, the feature does not).

For the technical detail behind each answer, read [the security guide](security.md). For the delivery modes, read [delivery modes](delivery-modes.md). For the steps your administrator follows, read [the organization setup guide](setup.md#organization-setup-for-private-drafting).

| Question | Answer | Benefit to you | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Do my confidential documents end up in the project repository? | No. Restricted originals and full renderings stay in your authorized document system or in a private owner workspace. The project repository holds only outputs you approved for everyone who can read it, and its checks pass from a clean clone without the originals. Originals are optional even when a document is public. **Supported.** | Nothing beyond what you approved enters the repository, its history, forks or every engineer's clone. | An output you approve for repository readers is, by that decision, readable by all of them. Ignore rules stop new files from being tracked. They do not erase what an earlier commit already holds. |
| When am I asked what is confidential? | Once, at the start, before any document is rendered or any staffing question is asked: can everyone who can read this repository see all of these documents and your staffing notes? Your answer is recorded per document. Carrydown compares the recorded source versions, the policy and the scope with the last release each time you preview, and tells you what changed. It cannot see a change to a document's permissions in your own document system, so that part is yours to watch. **Supported.** | One clear decision up front instead of a surprise at publish time. | The answer drives everything downstream, so it needs to be right. |
| If I answer yes, what changes for me? | Almost nothing. You draft where you do now. Your staffing notes go to a private record instead of the plan file, originals are no longer required in the repository, and new checks appear as ordinary checker findings. Before anything is shared you answer one more line, the disclosure approval, under a four-question preview. **Supported.** | The simple workflow stays simple. | Answering yes means every reader of the repository may see every document. Approval on this path is your own local confirmation. It is recorded as not authenticated, and no protected claim is made for it. |
| If I answer no, what changes for me? | Carrydown creates an owner-only, versioned workspace on your machine. All drafting happens there, including for documents you call shareable. Before uploading a candidate it checks who can read the private staging repository, or requires a current administrator attestation. A trusted workflow opens the review request so you are not its author. You approve the final revision in GitHub. Carrydown verifies that approval before it exports the approved outputs into an isolated branch of the engineering repository. **Supported with prerequisites.** Not yet proven on real GitHub. | Unapproved drafts and private source and staffing records do not enter engineering branches or clones. | Your organization installs the independent verifier and enrolls the repositories. Missing or unverifiable required controls stop the protected process before the private upload, not merely after it. Staging reviewers and service identities must also be authorized to see candidate metadata. |
| Where do people's names, leave dates and allocations go? | Into a private, versioned record that only you hold, on your machine. The committed plan carries only ids, tracks and decisions. GitHub logins for assignments are resolved when you prepare a release and are part of what you approve. Publishing who owns an item and publishing how much of someone's time it takes are separate permissions. **Supported.** | Engineers and repository readers see the decision, not the personal facts behind it. | Capacity and date checks run only on your machine. If you chose to delete raw inputs after a release, the export deletes the files in the workspace's `sources/` folder once the release is active and exported, and records each path, size and digest in `retention-log.jsonl`. It cannot remove copies in backups, the workspace's git history or earlier clones. Your agent's own transcript needs the retention rules your organization sets. |
| What does my engineer's coding agent actually receive? | One bundle per work item: the exact sections of your documents that item cites, the conflict decisions that apply, and the rules on what may appear in code, tests, issue text and evidence. Nothing from unrelated sections. The installed skill runs a convenience check that the bundle is intact and current before the agent reads it. That check lives in the engineer's own tools, so it is not the security control. The controls are the release gate on your exports and the independent verifier on pull requests, neither of which an engineer can edit. **Supported.** | Smaller, exact context for the agent, and no browsing of documents it does not need. | Carrydown checks that every cited obligation is present. Whether the obligations are sufficient is your review. Every reader of the repository can read every bundle, so leaving a section out of one bundle hides nothing from a teammate. |
| Can a derived file end up less protected than the document it came from? | No. Every derived artifact is checked against the audience you approve for it. A restricted source can feed a release only for an exact audience, target and set of files that you approve, and the preflight refuses a destination whose readers are broader than that audience. Mechanical detectors refuse credentials, private paths and staffing fields in any release. A copied passage or an organization phrase can be released only by an exception you approve by name. **Supported.** | Nothing is quietly downgraded by a script. | More approvals when many derivatives exist. Detectors reduce risk. They do not prove safety. |
| Are headings, file names and hashes safe to publish? | Not automatically. They are part of the files and payloads you approve, so the preview shows them and your approval covers them. Approval and integrity are verified before any bundle content is written. **Supported.** | No outline or filename leaks by accident. | Slightly more to approve. A published identifier or hash is a deliberate disclosure. |
| What proves that what engineers see is what I approved? | A content manifest identifies the exact output bytes. A separate approval request identifies who may receive them, where and how they may be used. You approve the final revision, including the fields of the small public receipt. Carrydown and the verifier regenerate that receipt from approved records and reject altered content or scope. Reapproving identical content creates a different request without overwriting history. **Supported.** Authenticated approval on the private path is **supported with prerequisites**. Not yet proven on real GitHub. | You can show exactly what was approved, by whom, for whom and where. | Private reviewer and staging details stay private. On the simple path approval is local and explicitly not authenticated. |
| Could a developer change the content and the checker together so it passes? | Not for a protected release. The local checker gives feedback only. The independent verifier reads current protected policy and separately pinned code from your organization's tooling repository. It has its own credential, never runs proposed code, and reports App-specific required checks. Deleting the project policy pointer cannot remove enrollment. **Supported with prerequisites.** Not yet proven on real GitHub. | A change cannot approve itself or borrow the verifier's credential. | Your organization operates and protects that environment. Runtime access restrictions are its responsibility, not something a repository checker can establish. |
| How quickly does independent verification notice a change? | It reevaluates every open request on a schedule that asks for a run every five minutes, and it can be started by hand. It rechecks unchanged commits when approval, policy, state or the base branch changes. A previous failure can also turn into a success after approval. **Supported with prerequisites.** Not yet proven on real GitHub. | Approval and withdrawal are not cached forever merely because no one pushed another commit. | Five minutes is a requested interval, not a deadline. GitHub can delay or drop scheduled runs. GitHub does not expire an old success during an outage, and state can change between a poll and a merge. Fresh authorization enforced at the exact merge needs an additional control that your organization runs. This deployment does not supply it. |
| What goes into GitHub issues and the board? | Only allowlisted fields approved for the actual repository and board identities. Publication checks their effective audience, not just whether they are called private. An approved public derivative of a confidential source is allowed at its named public target. A repository-reader release cannot be published to a public repository. Interrupted runs resume from the saved list of what was published, and a managed field that someone changed on GitHub stops the run for your decision. **Supported.** Not yet proven on real GitHub. | Important context reaches the intended audience without silently changing destination or publishing private staffing capacity. | GitHub has no issue-level permissions. Board metadata can have a different audience from linked issues. Unverifiable board access needs a current scoped administrator attestation or publication stops. Earlier disclosures cannot be undone. |
| What if implementing a rule would reveal it in code or tests? | You decide at approval time what each bundle may be used for: code, tests, issue text, and whether evidence may include real data or synthetic data only. Evidence uses synthetic data by default. An item whose implementation would disclose the rule can be kept out of the release as a restricted item. **Supported.** | No impossible assignments. Engineers know what they may write down before they start. | This is a decision and review control, not a scanner. Carrydown cannot detect every implication in code or prose. |
| What happens to work that cannot be shared with the team? | It stays in your private plan. The engineering plan carries at most the one-line blocker summary you wrote, shown as an external item, and optionally a short instruction you wrote for an engineer who names the item. No private title, description or link is published, and nothing is posted to GitHub when the item is done. **Supported.** | Only the blocker information you approved reaches the team. | Publishing the item to a different restricted repository is planned. An engineer receives a restricted-destination message only when you approved such a placeholder. Otherwise the item has no published context, and an unknown id and a withheld id get the same answer. |
| Does a private repository protect me? | Partly. Repository permissions provide an access boundary for their authorized readers. They do not separate those readers from each other, and they cannot recall copies already cloned. | A useful control with stated limits. | Material that some repository readers must not see needs a planned delivery mode, not a private repository. |
| What do I run and what does my organization run? | Carrydown supplies the software, the formats, a reference deployment and the tests. Your organization operates identity, permissions, approval authority and the trusted environment, on managed infrastructure if it wishes. | Fits controls you already have. No new hosted service to trust. | Without the organization's part, the protected path is not available and carrydown says so. |
| What if a document changes after a release? | You preview the change, which names the affected work items. You approve a replacement or a withdrawal. Reviewed state events preserve the history. A separate receipt-only operation can publish a withdrawal while every content publisher refuses the withdrawn approval. After the receipt reaches the engineering default branch, the engineer's convenience check sees it on refresh and stops old work. Approved expiry and offline grace are checked separately. **Supported.** | A withdrawal can be communicated without authorizing more publication of the withdrawn content. | A private source edit is not automatically known to engineers. Receipt publication and refresh take time, and an offline clone cannot learn a change. The first use in a clone needs a successful refresh before offline grace is available. Text already delivered, in clones, forks, transcripts or stored copies, cannot be recalled. Immediate revocation is not an embedded-delivery promise. |
| What does an engineer see when the check cannot proceed? | One of four plain messages: no approved context for the item, the approved context is stale, the item cites an obligation that is not in its context, or the item has a restricted destination that the project does not publish. The message never prints withheld text, restricted headings, private paths or approver names. **Supported.** | The engineer knows what to do next without learning what is withheld. | The check is a convenience in the engineer's own checkout and can be edited. It is not an access boundary. |
| Do hosted coding agents work? | Agents that run in a hosted environment can read the bundles from the repository, so they work without a local workspace. The freshness refresh is a `git fetch` of the default branch, and whether a hosted environment allows it depends on the host. If the fetch fails, the check applies the offline grace rule or stops. **Supported with prerequisites.** | No separate store or service to reach before work starts. | Carrydown's tests do not run inside any hosted agent environment, so confirm the fetch behavior for yours. A hosted environment is also part of the runtime-isolation attestation that your organization makes. |
| Does the rendering keep a document's "Confidential" marking? | Yes for Word documents. Header and footer markings are carried into the rendering, and a marking that disagrees with your classification stops the process until you decide. Hidden and tracked text is surfaced for your review rather than silently included or dropped. Links with schemes other than `http` and `https` are removed, and comments in Markdown sources are stripped. **Supported.** | Classification survives rendering. | Detection reduces risk. It is not immunity to malicious content elsewhere in a document. Markdown and text sources carry no header or footer marking to read. |
| Can logs leak what the checks are meant to hide? | Carrydown's checks print identifiers, field names, line numbers and fixed reason codes, never the matched text. [The security guide](security.md#the-release-decision) lists every reason code, including `schema-invalid`, an addition to the specification's list that means a record does not match its schema. Only the command that shows review items prints source text, and only to the console of the person who ran it. **Supported.** | Fewer side channels. | Debugging is less convenient. Your terminal history and your agent's transcript are outside carrydown. |
| How does an engineer question a rule? | Where the project asks for requests, the skill tells the agent to draft a `Rule clarification request` comment on the work item and to post it only with the engineer's permission. The generated skill holds no token code, so the engineer or an authorized tool posts it. The plan owner releases a revised bundle through a new request that replaces the old one. The previous approval and its reasons are kept. **Supported.** | Approved text is never silently edited. | A little more latency on clarifications. Where the project allows it, engineers may instead append to "Decisions recorded", and the next release reassesses. |
| I used carrydown before this. Where do I stand? | Your project is labeled legacy until you run the inventory, which lists tracked originals, renderings, staffing files and likely history exposure, with guidance. Nothing is deleted or rewritten for you. **Supported.** | You learn where you stand instead of assuming a later ignore rule fixed it. | Carrydown cannot remove history. GitHub's guidance on removing sensitive data applies. |
| How is any of this proven? | Tests cover unapproved export, altered bytes or scope, absence of private workspace and staging locators and withheld source content in engineering outputs, declared expiry and withdrawal behavior, a modified checker's inability to authorize itself, and maintenance bypasses. A recorded real GitHub acceptance run is meant to prove the one-owner review flow and the required checks. That run has not been performed. | Claims are tied to tested behavior, with documentation and sample checks in the release criteria. | Configuration separation does not prove an engineer's runtime cannot read a known private path. The organization must establish and attest to runtime isolation. These tests are a minimum, not proof of complete security. |
| Which delivery modes exist? | Embedded delivery, where approved material lives in the engineering repository for everyone who can read it, is **supported** for drafting from inputs approved for repository readers. Private drafting followed by approved embedded delivery is **supported with prerequisites**. Restricted stored delivery to a narrower group, brokered retrieval with per-person checks and an audit log, and restricted publication across repositories are **planned**. | Honest labels your security team can evaluate. | Planned means the design exists and the feature does not. |

Carrydown enforces its supported release process within an organization-controlled trust boundary. The organization is responsible for establishing and maintaining that boundary. Carrydown cannot prevent an authorized person or an unrestricted agent from copying information through unrelated tools outside that process.
